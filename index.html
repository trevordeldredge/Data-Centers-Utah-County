The basics

The internet has a physical footprint.

When you stream a show, save photos to the cloud, use a map app, or ask an AI tool a question, computers in data centers help process and store the information. The buildings also need power, cooling, fiber connections, backup systems, and people to operate them.[1]

Different centers serve different customers and workloads. A campus may host cloud and business services, AI computing, or government workloads; the operator and tenant do not always publish the exact use of each building.[1]

Servers and storage systems keep data available for people, companies, and institutions.[1]

Power equipment, cooling, security, and backup systems support around-the-clock service.[1]

A fair look at both sides

Benefits have a place in the conversation. So do the costs.

Data centers can create value and demand public resources at the same time. The questions below are designed to make both sides visible.

↗ What communities may gain

Possible benefits depend on the project, its customers, local agreements, and whether promised outcomes are delivered.

Digital services and AI

Data centers provide computing for cloud storage, streaming, online banking, business systems, research, and AI. The Utah State survey describes these as common data-center uses; the tenant mix of an individual project may not be public.[1]

National security and government missions

Some data infrastructure supports intelligence and other government work. NSA Utah, in Bluffdale, supports NSA missions; it is a statewide example and is not in Utah County.[13]

Jobs and technical pathways

Meta reports 1,200 construction workers at peak and 300+ jobs once its Eagle Mountain campus is complete. QTS estimates 2,000 construction jobs and about 100 permanent positions. Construction roles are temporary; project operators describe permanent jobs as company estimates.[5][7]

Ask for permanent jobs, wages, and local hiring—not just construction peaks.

Investment and community contributions

Meta says it has invested more than $50 million in local infrastructure and contributed over $6.2 million to local schools and organizations. Those are company-reported figures; project agreements and public accounting help residents assess what is committed and delivered.[5]

What Utahns say about end use: In the Utah State survey, 43.9% said a local project serving the medical industry would make them more likely to support it; 9.6% said the same about a project supporting growing AI uses. This is a survey result, not a forecast of local approval.[1]

↘ What communities need to weigh

The scale and design determine impacts. Ask for project-level measurements, not generic industry averages.

Water in a dry region

Cooling design changes direct water use, but buildings may still need water for initial fills, maintenance, kitchens, fire protection, and landscaping. Power generation can also use water beyond the facility boundary.[1][3][17]

Power demand and emissions

Large electricity demand can require new generation, transmission, substations, or storage. Greenhouse-gas and air-quality impacts depend on the energy sources and on-site generators used. Renewable-energy matching claims do not replace reporting the site’s actual electricity demand and supply arrangement.[3][5]

Noise, light, heat, and land

Cooling equipment, backup generators, lighting, and construction can affect nearby residents. Large sites can also change agricultural land, wildlife habitat, views, and open space; county draft standards call for studies of several of these effects.[6]

Who pays—and who benefits?

Tax incentives, utility upgrades, roads, water systems, emergency response, and public services all affect the net community outcome. Ask for costs, revenue, and commitments in writing, with updates that residents can check later.[4][6]

What Utahns say they want: 88.8% of survey respondents favored ensuring the public can participate in and influence decisions; 85.1% favored completing impact studies before development.[1]
Design choices can reduce impacts

Better technology helps. Public numbers still matter.

These approaches can lower some impacts, but their performance should be checked against local conditions and reported results.

◉

Closed-loop, dry, or air cooling

Closed-loop systems recirculate coolant; dry and air-cooled systems can reduce water used for cooling. They still require initial fills, maintenance, and water for other building needs. Utah County’s recommended draft would require low-water cooling designs and prohibit once-through and high-loss evaporative cooling.[17]

Ask for initial fill + annual use
⚡

New generation, storage, and renewables

On-site generation, batteries, and renewable-energy purchases can shape how a facility is served. On-site gas generation can reduce dependence on grid supply while adding emissions, fuel, and permitting questions. A credible plan reports actual demand, energy sources, emissions, and who pays for upgrades.[5][17][19]

Ask for source + cost by phase
↻

Reuse and water stewardship

Reusing suitable water for irrigation or replacing water through restoration projects can help, but local withdrawal and consumption should still be reported separately. Meta reports 35.1 million gallons withdrawn at Eagle Mountain in 2024 and describes water-restoration work as part of its stewardship efforts.[3][5]

Ask for measured results, not only goals

A design promise is not the same as an operating result. Compare proposed estimates with annual measured use after a facility opens.[3][6][17]

Local process + state rules

How Utah County is responding to calls for transparency

The county’s proposal focuses on earlier notice, public access to project details, and studies of water, power, and environmental effects. State laws add water-reporting and large-load cost requirements.

County proposal · not adopted as of Nov. 23, 2026

What the recommended draft would require

  • At least two public open houses before a complete application for medium and larger projects; mailed notice to nearby property owners.
  • A public project website with location, size, capacity, schedule, approvals, contacts, public questions, and updates.
  • Water studies covering demand by season, source and rights, closed-loop initial fill, nearby wells, reuse, and water for on-site generation.
  • Disclosure of peak and average power demand, supply source, grid needs, utility upgrades, and the proposed cost allocation.
  • Noise, air-emissions, heat, lighting, traffic, and environmental analysis; financial assurance for decommissioning and site restoration.

This is a proposed land-use ordinance for unincorporated Utah County. It would not replace city rules or state and federal permits. The October 20 Planning Commission draft is a recommendation; county materials show the Commission was still working on the ordinance in November.[16][17][21]

State laws that add protections

HB 76 · Water transparency

New large centers that begin operating on or after July 1, 2026 and meet the law’s thresholds (at least 10,000 square feet and 75 acre-feet of annual water withdrawal) must report projected water use before construction and actual withdrawals annually. State rules publish withdrawal data by facility; certain other information can be protected and reported in aggregate. The law creates reporting, not a water-use cap or cooling mandate.[18]

SB 132 · Large-load electricity costs

For qualifying large loads, Utah law assigns reasonable incremental costs of service—such as generation and grid upgrades—to the customer responsible for the load, subject to the law’s terms and regulatory review.[19]

HB 507 · Incentives

Beginning May 6, 2027, local governments are generally restricted from offering incentives to large-load data centers, with statutory exceptions for specified development zones and funds.[20]

The county’s October 7 release said there were no pending applications for data centers in unincorporated Utah County at that time. The county proposal described here is a draft—not a current countywide ban or a final permit decision.[16]

Take part with specific questions
“What will this project use, what will it provide, and how will we know?”

That question works for any proposal—whether the answer is a company estimate, a permit requirement, or an audited operating result.

Power: What is the peak and average demand? What source supplies it? Who pays for new infrastructure?
Water: What is the initial fill, annual use, water source, discharge plan, and drought response?
Jobs: How many permanent local jobs are expected, at what wages, and how many are temporary construction jobs?
Neighborhood: What noise, lighting, air, heat, traffic, and wildlife studies are public—and who verifies them?
Accountability: What will be reported after opening, how often, and what happens if commitments are missed?
